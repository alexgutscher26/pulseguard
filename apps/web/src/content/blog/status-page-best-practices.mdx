A status page is the one piece of infrastructure users actually *want* to visit during an outage. Done well, it turns a bad moment into a trust-building one. Done poorly, it's the thing screenshots circulate on social media. Here's the playbook.

## Before the incident: set up for success

The time to think about outage communication is when everything is green.

- **Make the page findable** — link it from your footer, docs, and login screens. A status page nobody can find is a liability
- **Put the status page on its own infrastructure** — if it's hosted on the same stack as your product, it goes down with it
- **Use a custom domain** — `status.yourdomain.com` is more credible and memorable than a vendor subdomain
- **Subscribe users in advance** — pre-incident subscribers get updates automatically; emergency subscribers can't log in during an outage
- **Write templates now** — skeleton incident updates you can fill in under pressure

## During the incident: the three rules

1. **Acknowledge within minutes.** The first update should appear as soon as you've confirmed something is wrong — even if you don't know the cause yet. "We're investigating elevated error rates on the API" is a complete, honest update.
2. **Update on a schedule.** Users refresh status pages obsessively. Every 30–60 minutes without an update, they assume the worst. Set a reminder to post even when there's nothing new: "No change yet — investigation ongoing."
3. **Never claim more than you know.** Don't say "the issue is resolved" while your monitors still show failures. You can say "we're seeing recovery; validating before marking resolved."

## What a good update contains

| Element | Example |
| ------- | ------- |
| Affected systems | "Checkout and payment API" |
| Impact | "Users may see errors completing orders" |
| Started at | "14:32 UTC, confirmed at 14:40 UTC" |
| Current action | "Rolling back the deployment from 14:00" |
| Next update | "Next update within 30 minutes" |

Every update should answer the same four questions: what's affected, how badly, what are you doing, and when will we hear next.

## The status page anatomy

A status page is a hierarchy, not a wall of text:

- **Overall status** — one indicator at the top: Operational / Degraded / Major Outage
- **Component status** — per-service indicators (API, Web, Database, CDN)
- **Active incidents** — newest first, with severity and start time
- **Incident timeline** — the full update history, kept as a permanent record
- **Uptime history** — rolling 30/90-day uptime percentages per component

Put the overall indicator at the top, keep component granularity, and never bury the incident timeline.

## After the incident: post-mortems and history

- **Post-mortem within 72 hours** — publish root cause, timeline, and corrective actions. Ambitious companies publish even embarrassing post-mortems; users reward the honesty
- **Keep incident history** — incident pages should stay live forever. They're your credibility archive
- **Update your SLAs** — reconcile reported uptime against your [SLA calculations](/blog/sla-calculations) and issue credits where owed

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Saying "resolved" twice** — declaring resolution then reopening the incident erodes trust more than a slow incident
- **Markdown walls** — pasting internal chat logs into the status page. Write for users, not for yourself
- **Vendor-status-only monitoring** — if your status page is driven by your monitoring tool's checks, make sure those checks are multi-region; a single-node check will declare you down when you're not

## The bottom line

Users forgive outages. They don't forgive silence. A status page that acknowledges fast, updates on schedule, and publishes honest post-mortems converts every incident into a proof point — and that's the best any monitoring setup can do.
